    <title>GPWM: Modeling the World as 3D Particle Motion</title>
    <updated>2026-11-05T17:24:36Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08338v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08338v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>We introduce GPWM, a Generative Particle World Model that predicts how 3D scenes evolve by generating the motion of particles and rigid bodies over time. Unlike video world models that generate scene appearance, GPWM operates directly in 3D physical space. A single representation and architecture cover rigid, deformable, cloth, and granular materials, in both passive physics and robotic interaction. GPWM is an object-aware multi-resolution transformer that combines flow matching with diffusion forcing to generate coherent physical trajectories far beyond its training horizon. We train and evaluate GPWM on ParticleWorlds, a 25.5-million-frame corpus spanning diverse geometries, materials, interactions, and robot embodiments. GPWM outperforms the best prior learned-dynamics baseline in each of six physical domains, reducing prediction error by 31% on average. Breaking from the domain-specific setting of prior methods, a single Unified GPWM models these heterogeneous regimes with one set of weights, largely outperforming per-domain baselines and even surpassing our own specialist models in two domains. GPWM further generalizes to unseen geometries, physical properties, object counts, and rollout horizons. Beyond prediction, GPWM enables contact-rich planning by learning a generative prior over joint actuator-object motion. Instead of searching over unconstrained actions, planning searches the model's latent noise for interactions that achieve a desired object-space goal. With the same world model, noise-space planning substantially outperforms action-space search on pushing and picking, where complex contact sequences are hard to discover. Executed open-loop in simulation, the resulting plans achieve goal-directed manipulation without reference action trajectories or goal-specific policies.</summary>

    <title>Bouncing or Merging in Drop-Bath Impacts</title>
    <updated>2026-11-05T12:42:09Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07942v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.07942v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>When a liquid drop impacts a liquid surface, it deforms the surface, trapping a thin layer of air between drop and bath. The drop then either bounces off the bath or, if the gas film ruptures, merges with it. We present a model for drop-bath impacts that can accurately predict whether a drop will bounce or merge, and compare it to theory-driven experiments. The model incorporates a lubrication description of the deforming trapped gas film, which accounts for its significant curvature, within a finite-element framework. Capturing this outcome requires micro- and nano-scale physics: van der Waals-driven disjoining pressure and gas-kinetic effects that depend on the mean free path of the gas. Using only independently measured material parameters, we obtain the first quantitative agreement with experiment for the critical impact speed separating bounce from merge. We explore the behaviour of drop-bath impacts at low ambient air pressure, and then investigate the bounce-merge threshold for impacts onto shallow baths and thin films, finding a bounce-merge-bounce-merge transition as the bath depth decreases.</summary>

    <title>Structure from Collision via Hypothesis-Space Reasoning</title>
    <updated>2026-11-05T12:20:11Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07900v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.07900v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Knowledge of the hidden interior structure of an object, including the presence and location of cavities, is essential for predictive simulation, robotic manipulation, and nondestructive inspection. However, such cavities leave no direct signature on the static visible surface, making collision-induced deformation one of the few cues used to infer their presence. Structure from Collision (SfC) addresses this problem by recovering the hidden interior structure of an object from multiview observations of a collision event. SfC is challenging for two reasons: hidden cavities are only indirectly observable through surface motion, and voids correspond to the absence of material, making direct optimization using standard density representations difficult. The density-based SfC baseline inherits both difficulties. Surface-matching gradients do not directly indicate the cavity location, while density gradients can be weak or uninformative inside or near void regions. Therefore, we reformulate SfC as hypothesis-space reasoning, representing hidden cavities as explicit structural hypotheses whose variables encode available structural assumptions rather than relying on implicit density updates. A global stage performs gradient-free optimization over this hypothesis space using forward-only material point method (MPM) simulation. A local stage then refines the selected hypothesis as a Lagrangian particle system through gradient-based optimization using differentiable MPM simulation. On the SfC benchmark, our method improves hidden-structure reconstruction and future prediction over the density-based SfC baseline. Interior-topology stress tests and robustness analyses characterize its scope and limitations. The project page is available at https://www.kecl.ntt.co.jp/people/kaneko.takuhiro/projects/sfc-hypothesis/.</summary>

    <summary>Deformable linear object (DLO) manipulation is challenging because high-dimensional configurations, nonlinear contact dynamics, and diverse material and geometric properties produce a broad range of object responses that are both difficult to model and data-intensive to learn. This work studies reinforcement learning for planar manipulation of a one-end-grasped DLO, focusing on training efficiency, observation design, robustness, and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. We compare three Proximal Policy Optimization training stacks and find that the GPU-parallel MuJoCo Playground stack enables faster convergence and more consistent performance than CPU-based alternatives under the same wall-clock budget.     <title>Fluidic Body: Swarm Robots as a Fluid Extension of the Hand</title>
    <updated>2026-11-04T07:13:34Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06068v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.06068v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Swarm robots have attracted growing interest as a physical interface for human--computer interaction, since a swarm can reshape its formation to fit the objects and surroundings it works with. For a human operator, however, adapting the swarm's behavior remains difficult. Most existing methods require the user to specify where the swarm should go, and they offer no intuitive way to change the collective's behavior as each task demands. We present Fluidic Body, a system that lets a person operate a swarm of fluid-imitating robots as an extension of the hand. The system places a virtual fluid between the hand and the swarm, and the user adapts the swarm's behavior to each task by changing the fluid's properties through simple hand movements. When the user opens the hand, the swarm becomes soft enough to deform and flow around obstacles. Closing the hand turns it into a hard, cohesive body that can push objects. We evaluated the controller's performance and its operability in two simulation experiments and one demonstration with physical robots. First, our controller handled both obstacle avoidance and object transportation better than an existing method that behaves the same way in every task. Second, in a VR user study, 17 participants completed the combined avoidance-and-transportation task faster and more reliably than with that method. They also reported a lower workload and a stronger sense of agency. Finally, this experience carried over to physical robots in a real-world demonstration (88 participants). These results suggest that treating a swarm as a fluid, a material people already understand, allows a robot swarm to work as a deformable extension of the human body.</summary>

    <title>Cool Graphs: Active Property Search Towards Quantum Nano-Refrigerators</title>
    <updated>2026-11-03T13:50:16Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04907v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04907v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Nanodevices that absorb energy from their thermal environment can act as quantum refrigerators. Finding suitable configurations, however, is a needle-in-a-haystack problem: the many-body state space scales exponentially with the size of the device, where each candidate configuration requires an expensive quantum-chemical computation. We introduce a search framework that couples a Graph Variational Autoencoder (GVAE) with an uncertainty-driven Active Learning (AL) loop to efficiently identify energy-absorbing configurations in electronic junctions with a 2D "working material" to be optimized. The GVAE maps physical parameters - site energies, coupling matrices, temperature - into a smooth latent space that distinguishes cooling from dissipative parameter regimes, achieving a classification discriminability of $0.920 \pm 0.004$ on held-out configurations and $0.895 \pm 0.010$ on unseen extrapolation topologies. An AL pipeline based on Monte Carlo dropout then targets the decision boundary, iteratively selecting the candidates where the model is least certain for simulation. Over 32 AL cycles, the uncertainty-driven strategy discovers energy absorbing systems at rates consistently exceeding the random baseline across all tested topologies (where only 1.9-8.4% of configurations exhibit the desired property), providing a data-efficient pathway for navigating a combinatorically large design space. The learned latent space organizes configurations along physically interpretable axes, that correlate with underlying microscopic model parameters. This hints at the existence of "cooling coordinates" - low-dimensional, physically interpretable quantities separating cooling from non-cooling regimes. Our framework, thus, paves a new way towards effective cooling of nanoscale electronic information processing devices, to reduce the environmental footprint of high performance computing.</summary>

    <summary>We introduce iMED, the first synchronized multi-endoscope dataset for robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RAMIS), designed to address a fundamental limitation in surgical vision benchmarking: the absence of independent held-out viewpoints. Existing surgical datasets capture single-trajectory sequences from a single endoscope, making it impossible to distinguish geometric generalization from photometric interpolation along a narrow forward-facing path. iMED provides 340 sequences ~170K synchronized timepoints with 4 views per timepoint) recorded simultaneously from two independent stereo endoscopes across ex vivo, postmortem, and live surgical settings, spanning 14 specimens with diverse anatomical regions and motion regimes. Notably, large-scale foundation models transfer surprisingly well to surgical imagery, often outperforming domain-specific models, suggesting pitfalls in current fine-tuning protocols. iMED establishes a new evaluation protocol for geometric generalization in surgical vision.</summary>

    <summary>Learning robust manipulation policies for diverse everyday objects remains challenging because objects with different materials exhibit distinct deformation and contact dynamics. While diffusion policies provide a powerful framework for modeling multimodal manipulation behaviors, they typically lack explicit physical reasoning and may produce averaged actions when facing heterogeneous objects. Our framework recovers material properties from visual demonstrations by coupling 3D Gaussian Splatting with differentiable Material Point Method simulation, where video guidance and particle-level constraints enable accurate physical parameter estimation. These results show that incorporating physical knowledge is critical for scalable robot manipulation in diverse environments.</summary>

    <title>Contrasting Eulerian and Lagrangian organization of turbulent dissipation</title>
    <updated>2026-11-02T12:25:08Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03029v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.03029v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>The dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy is conventionally attributed to a scale-invariant spatial multiplicative cascade, with Lagrangian statistics inferred through bridge relations. Direct numerical simulations with tracers reveal instead that Eulerian coarse-grained dissipation has a squared-logarithmic cascade depth and scale-dependent multipliers, whereas time-averaged dissipation along trajectories displays multifractal scaling and an approximately scale-invariant temporal cascade. The classical bridge fails at the level of individual trajectories. Eulerian multipliers nevertheless possess a multifractal organization whose accumulation accounts for the squared-logarithmic spatial statistics. This contrast motivates a material-inheritance picture in which Eulerian dissipation statistics reflect the spatial assembly, deformation, and mixing of distinct Lagrangian histories. The results therefore distinguish the temporal cascade organization revealed along fluid trajectories from the non-stationary spatial refinement observed in the Eulerian frame.</summary>

    <title>From Rock Tumbling to Telescope Mirror Grinding: How Optical Perfection Emerges from Random Motion</title>
    <updated>2026-11-01T07:39:12Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01673v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>

    <summary>When two glass disks are rubbed together by hand with an abrasive between them, their surfaces spontaneously converge to perfectly matching spherical curves, accurate to tens of nanometers. How does such optical precision emerge from an essentially random manual process? Starting from Preston's law of material removal, we derive a first-principles explanation. Symmetries of the grinding stroke imply that planes and spheres are the generic equilibria of the process. Their stability is governed by a hierarchy of timescales: small-scale irregularities decay exponentially on the grinding timescale $T_\text{grind}$, while large-scale deviations from a sphere diffuse away on the longer spherizing timescale $T_\text{sphere} \propto (R_\text{mirror}/R_\text{stroke})^2\, T_\text{grind}$. The same framework unifies telescope mirror grinding with everyday phenomena: rock tumbling (explaining why rotary tumbling rounds edges more than vibratory, and why neither produce spheres) and sanding (explaining why small flat surfaces inevitably round). These examples offer an accessible classroom context for stable and unstable equilibria, exponential relaxation, diffusion, multi-timescale analysis, and the emergence of the Laplacian operator from averaging over a local area.</summary>

    <summary>Robotic surface exploration benefits from tactile interfaces that accommodate contact and capture variations in surface characteristics. Herein, we report MagPillar, a tactile sensor inspired by spider tactile hairs that combines a magnetic pillar array with three-axis Hall sensing. A full-array mechanical and magnetic-field model characterizes how pillar deformation affects the superposed fields measured across the array, providing a physical basis for interpreting its contact responses. The compliant pillar architecture translates contact with surface textures and geometric features into distinct magnetic response patterns. Then, in robotic surface perception experiments, spectral features and logistic regression distinguish six material surfaces with an offline accuracy of 99.34% under within-session temporally blocked cross-validation. Continuous scans over periodic protrusions produce amplitude and peak-spacing variations consistent with changes in surface geometry. These findings connect compliant contact mechanics with distributed magnetic sensing, paving a new path for material recognition and qualitative surface-profile perception.</summary>
